Consent Form Summary:

This research study aims to see if an investigational vaccine, PRGN-2009, improves the immune system’s response to treatment in people newly diagnosed with certain HPV-related cancers – specifically oropharyngeal or sinonasal cancers. You’ll receive the HPV vaccine through injections and have follow-up tests like blood work, scans, and potentially a tumor biopsy. Participation involves regular visits over about one month, followed by five years of check-ins to monitor your health. While there’s no guarantee of benefit, this study could help future patients. Potential side effects include fatigue, fever, headache, and muscle aches, but serious complications are possible. Your standard cancer treatment will continue as prescribed by your doctor and won’t be affected by this study. Your medical information will be kept private, though it may be shared for research purposes under strict protections. You won’t receive direct payment, but travel and lodging expenses may be reimbursed. You can stop participating at any time without penalty. If you experience an injury related to the study, the NIH will provide initial medical care. If you have questions or concerns, contact Dr. Charalampos Floudas at 240-858-3032 or charalampos.floudas@nih.gov.

## What are the risks of radiation from being in the study?

During your participation in this research study, you will be exposed to radiation from 3 CT scans. The amount of radiation exposure you will receive from these procedures is equal to approximately 0.99 rem or 1.29 rem depening on your type of cancer. A rem is a unit of absorbed radiation.

Every  day,  people  are  exposed  to  low  levels  of  radiation  that  come  from  the  sun  and  the environment around them. The average person in the United States receives a radiation exposure of 0.3 rem per year from these sources. This type of radiation is called 'background radiation.' No one knows for sure whether exposure to these low amounts of radiation is harmful to your body.

The CT scans that you get in this study will expose you to the roughly the same amount of radiation as 3.3 or 4.3 years' worth of background radiation, depending on your type of cancer. Most of the time, this amount of extra radiation is    not harmful to you. However, scientists believe that being exposed to too much radiation can cause harmful side effects. This could include gettinga new cancer. We estimate that this could happen in about 1 out of every 1000 people who get a very large amount of extra radiation.








You may not participate in this study if you are pregnant. If you are able to become pregnant, we will perform a pregnancy test before exposing you to radiation. You must tell us if you may have become pregnant within the previous 14 days because the pregnancy test is unreliable during that time.
